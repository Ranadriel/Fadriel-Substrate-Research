TheFuckery That Is Fuck

A word analysis. One syllable, every slot in the grammar,
and almost no meaning left.
— collected by Rana
fuck/fʌk/
  1. v. To copulate. (The original job. Mostly retired.)
  2. v. To ruin, cheat, or mishandle. fuck up, fuck over
  3. int. A noise made at the universe.
  4. n. A unit of care, issued in amounts of zero. not give a fuck
  5. intens. Anything, but louder.

The Mad Science of Nothing is a cabinet of real phenomena seen through one lens: absence as identity. This is the same cabinet with a single specimen on the shelf, examined from every side. Every fact here is documented: court rolls, ciphers, linguistics papers, ice-water experiments, Supreme Court opinions. The joke is the door. What's behind it holds weight.

The finding, stated up front so you can watch it get proven: the meaning of this word is the least important thing about it. Take the letters away and the meaning stays. Translate it and the meaning travels. Damage the brain and the meaning goes, but the word stays. The force was never in the meaning.

Specimen I

Where it came from — and where it didn't

It's Germanic, and it probably has cousins: Middle Dutch fokken (thrust, copulate; in modern Dutch, to breed livestock), Norwegian dialect fukka, Swedish dialect focka (copulate, strike, push), German ficken (once: to move briskly back and forth). The family resemblance is strong, though the Oxford English Dictionary won't swear to the bloodline. Read the senses together and a pattern shows: sex is a specialization of hitting.

Not an acronym. Never was. No acronym story turns up in print before the 1960s. The word is centuries older.

The written record is thin, because the word was taboo before it was common. What survives arrives sideways: as a nickname in a court roll, in cipher, in a margin.

  • 1310Chester's county court summons a man three times, then outlaws him. The clerk writes him down as Roger Fuckebythenavele. Found in 2015 by the Keele historian Paul Booth. It's almost certainly an insult nickname: either a hopeless lover or just a dimwit. Possibly the earliest clearly sexual use.
  • c.1475“Flen flyys,” a Latin-English poem mocking the Carmelite friars of Cambridge. The dangerous word is hidden in a shift cipher. Decoded, the line says the friars fuck the wives of Ely, with a pun on hell.
  • c.1503The Scots poet William Dunbar, in “In secreit place this hyndir nycht,” rhymes fukkit with chukkit (fondled). In plain text. Scotland was less shy.
  • 1528A monk scribbles O d fuckin Abbot in the margin of a copy of Cicero's De Officiis. The d is usually read as “damned.” It's the earliest known adjective use.
  • 1535The modern spelling, fuck, appears in the work of the Scots poet David Lyndsay.
⚙ Friar-Proof Cipher, c.1475
Each letter moved one place forward in the scribe's alphabet: no w, and u/v and i/j counted as one letter each. That's why x steps back to v.

Look at the 1528 margin. Almost as soon as the word shows up in writing, it's already working as an intensifier for something other than sex. The emptying started early.

Specimen II

Every slot in the grammar — the promiscuous word

Most English words have one or two parts of speech. This one gets into all of them. The same four letters, unchanged, in every slot:

verbThey fucked it up.
nounI don't give a fuck.
adjectiveWhere are my fucking keys?
adverbThat's fucking brilliant.
interjectionFuck!
phrasal verbfuck off · fuck around · fuck with · fuck over — four separate meanings
wh-intensifierWhat the fuck is that?
degree nounGet the fuck out. · Cold as fuck.
infixabsofuckinglutely
whole sentenceFuck. (Complete. Nothing missing.)

Look at the fuck in “get the fuck out.” It's a noun phrase that names nothing. Take it out and the sentence means exactly the same thing, only quieter. It's grammatical packing material: a slot filled with an object that isn't there.

Specimen III

“Fuck you” is not a command — the subjectless sentence

On February 5, 1967, a linguistics paper began to circulate under the name Quang Phuc Dong, of the fictional “South Hanoi Institute of Technology.” It was really James McCawley, and his point was serious: fuck you looks like an order, like close the door, but it fails every test for one.

negate itDon't close the door. ✓
*Don't fuck you. ✗
soften itPlease close the door. ✓
*Please fuck you. ✗
threaten itClose the door or I'll take away your teddy-bear. ✓
*Fuck you or I'll take away your teddy-bear. ✗
join itWash the dishes and sweep the floor. ✓
*Wash the dishes and fuck you. ✗
report itI told you to close the door. ✓
*I told you to fuck you. ✗

Fuck you works like damn you or to hell with you. It's a fixed curse with no subject, no verb doing verb things, and no instruction inside it. Nobody is being told to do anything. The sentence has the shape of a command with the command removed: its grammar is a gesture. The paper was published in 1971 in a joke-titled festschrift, Studies Out in Left Field, and it's still cited.

Specimen IV

The infix machine — abso-fucking-lutely

English barely has infixes, meaning pieces inserted inside a word. Expletive infixation is the main exception, and it obeys a strict rule that nobody was taught. In 1982 the linguist John McCarthy wrote it down: the infix goes immediately before a stressed foot, with a complete foot on either side of it. A foot is a unit of rhythm, one stressed beat plus whatever unstressed syllables lean on it.

So fan-fucking-tastic and Ala-fucking-bama work (both straight from McCarthy's examples), while fanta-fucking-stic splits a foot in half and sounds wrong. You already know the rule. Test it: tap the gap where it belongs.

The point: the infix adds no meaning at all. It's pure stress, inserted wherever stress is about to land. A word with nothing left to say still keeps perfect time.

Specimen V

The sound of swearing — what's missing from the mouth

Is there a sound that makes a word profane? The psychologists Shiri Lev-Ari and Ryan McKay asked speakers of Hebrew, Hindi, Hungarian, Korean, and Russian for their worst swear words, then looked at the phonetics. The popular guess, that swears are packed with hard p, t, k sounds, didn't hold up beyond English and its relatives.

What did hold up was an absence. Across those languages, swear words tend to lack the approximants: the soft, flowing sounds l, r, w, y. Then they tested it the other way: 215 speakers of Arabic, Chinese, Finnish, French, German, and Spanish heard pairs of words from languages they didn't know. Asked to guess which was the swear, they picked the one with no approximant 63% of the time.

And the softened stand-ins go the other way: minced oaths like darn for damn carry about twice as many approximants as the words they replace. To defang a swear, you put the soft sounds back in.

Fuck: f, ʌ, k. Not an approximant in sight. Profanity is identified by what its mouth leaves out.

Rough cut: it reads spelling, not sound, so a silent or doubled letter can fool it. The real study measured sounds.

Specimen VI

Semantic bleaching — how the word emptied out

Linguists call it bleaching: use a word hard enough and it stops meaning what it means and just carries force. Very comes from Old French verai, “true”; by the late 1300s it was a pure intensifier. Awfully once meant “in a way that inspires awe”; since about 1830 it's meant “very.” Fuck went further than either.

In fucking brilliant there's no sex, and nothing bad either. The word is an amplifier. It points at the next word and says more. You can hold a whole conversation in it, each use meaning something different, and the listener decodes it entirely from pitch, timing, and context. The word is a carrier wave. The information rides on the tone.

Specimen VII

The asterisk — censorship by absence

Censorship tries to remove the word. It can't, because the word was never in the letters. Run the deletion yourself:

⚙ Progressive Deletion Apparatus
meaning: sting:

Two findings sit underneath that dial, and they're the hinge of this whole analysis.

The sting lives in the form. In 2011 the psychologists John Bowers and Christopher Pleydell-Pearce had 24 people read words aloud while their skin conductance (a measure of physical arousal) was recorded. The real swear words produced a bigger response than the F-word and the C-word, even though everyone knew exactly what those stood for. Neutral words and their stand-ins showed no difference. Same meaning, less body. The authors called it a first attempt; it hasn't been widely replicated.

Delete it entirely and the gap fills with worse. When the White House released edited Watergate transcripts on April 29, 1974, every profanity was replaced with [expletive deleted], down to mild ones like hell. Readers assumed Nixon's language was far filthier than it was. The absence carried more charge than the word would have.

Novelists had been running the same experiment for decades. Norman Mailer's publisher had him print fug in The Naked and the Dead (1948). Nobody reading it ever wondered what it meant.

Specimen VIII

The word abroad — meaning travels, sting stays home

Every language keeps its heaviest taboo somewhere different, and fuck shows how local that weight is.

Quebec FrenchThe church. Tabarnak (tabernacle), câlice (chalice), ostie (the communion host). These outrank sex and toilet words; tabarnak is usually the worst. About twenty base words spawn some four hundred softened variants.
DutchDisease. Kanker (cancer), tyfus (typhoid), tering (tuberculosis). Wishing an illness on someone is the heavy artillery.
Spanish, SerbianThe mother. Insults that drag the most sacred family figure into filth draw their force from family honour.
EnglishSex and the body, with fuck as the workhorse.

Now export the English word. A German film was titled Fack ju Göhte (roughly “Fuck you, Goethe,” spelled the way a German teenager would). The EU trademark office refused to register it as against public morality, and the General Court agreed in 2018. In February 2020 the EU's Court of Justice overturned both. Its reasoning: German speakers don't necessarily hear the English phrase the way they'd hear the same words in German. It even noted that the Goethe-Institut had used the film to teach.

The meaning crossed the border intact. Every German knows what it says. The sting didn't make the trip. Same result as the asterisk, from the opposite direction.

Specimen IX

The painkiller — ice water at Keele

In 2009 Richard Stephens and colleagues at Keele University had 67 students hold a hand in ice water for as long as they could stand it (capped at five minutes). Once while repeating a swear word of their choice, once while repeating a neutral word.

Swearing bought roughly 40 extra seconds, about a third more endurance. People also rated the pain lower while swearing.

The follow-ups are where it gets interesting for this analysis:

  • 2011Habituation. The more someone swears in daily life, the less swearing helps them in the ice water. Overuse drains the charge. Bleaching, measured in seconds.
  • 2020Made-up swears don't work. Ninety-two people tried fuck against two invented words, fouch and twizpipe. People found the fake words funny and even arousing, but only fuck helped: it raised pain threshold by 32% and tolerance by 33%. The fakes did nothing.
  • 2024The scorecard. A review of six studies found the tolerance effect reliable. The heart-rate rise seen in 2009 hasn't replicated, and the effect on how much it hurts is mixed. partly contested

So the painkiller isn't the sound (fouch sounds close enough) and it isn't any particular meaning (nobody in the ice water is talking about sex). It's the charge: the taboo the word has built up over a lifetime of being forbidden. Swear too often and you spend it.

Specimen X

The word that survives the stroke — swearing below language

Brain damage runs the deletion experiment in reverse: it strips away the language and leaves the swear standing.

  • 1861Paul Broca's famous patient lost nearly all his speech. Among the few things he could still produce was a curse: Sacré nom de Dieu.
  • 1980sThe neurolinguist Diana Van Lancker Sidtis worked with a North Dakota farmer who'd lost his language to a left-hemisphere stroke and couldn't hold a conversation about the weather. She whispered “Ronald Reagan.” He answered: goddammit.

Her explanation: building new sentences is mostly a left-hemisphere job, but fixed, ready-made expressions, like curses, counting, and the days of the week, are stored differently. The right hemisphere and the basal ganglia (deep structures that run habits) may keep a swear as a single unit, more like a reflex than a sentence. Not everyone accepts the right-hemisphere part. contested

Coprolalia, the involuntary swearing associated with Tourette syndrome, is rarer than its reputation. A 2009 study of 597 patients across seven countries found it in 19% of males and 15% of females at some point in their lives. It usually starts around age eleven, about five years after the tics. Brain imaging during these tics shows activity in the limbic system, the brain's emotional machinery, not the language centres.

And the legend: Phineas Gage, the railroad foreman who survived an iron rod through his skull in 1848. His doctor wrote twenty years later that Gage had taken to “the grossest profanity.” Accounts from closer to the time were much milder, and historians think the story grew in the retelling. contested

Put the cases together: the swear lives beneath language, closer to a flinch than a sentence. When the meaning-making machinery breaks, the word is still there, because it was never stored with the meanings.

#1
By 2014 fuck had overtaken bloody as the most common swear in British conversation.

The British numbers come from two large recorded collections of everyday speech, one from 1994 and one from 2014. Over those twenty years swearing overall fell, from about 2,265 to 1,428 per million words. Fewer swears in total, and more of them the same one. The vocabulary of profanity is consolidating around a single word, which is exactly what you'd expect of a word that can fill any slot.

Britain's broadcast regulator, Ofcom, asked 368 people in 2021 to rate 186 words. Fuck came out “strong,” not the worst. Older people rated it as more offensive than younger people did. The charge is draining in real time.

Specimen XII

The word in print — a ledger of permissions

  • 1795–1965The word appears in no general English dictionary. For 170 years the record of the language had a hole exactly this shape.
  • 1933Judge John M. Woolsey rules that Ulysses may enter the United States: in places “somewhat emetic,” he finds, but nowhere an aphrodisiac.
  • 1948Mailer's fug.
  • 1951James Jones's From Here to Eternity prints the word about fifty times, down from 258 in his manuscript.
  • 1960R v Penguin Books, London. The prosecutor in the Lady Chatterley's Lover trial counts the word at least thirty times and asks the jury whether it's a book they'd wish their wives or servants to read. The jury acquits unanimously. The first printing of 200,000 sells out in a day.
  • 1965The critic Kenneth Tynan says it on live BBC television, debating theatre censorship. No recording survives. It may not have been the first time on British TV. contested
  • 1972The Oxford English Dictionary supplement includes it. Its editor, Robert Burchfield, said he was proud to be the first OED editor to do so.
Specimen XIII

The word in court — trying to arrest a vacuum

  • 1971Cohen v. California. Paul Cohen wore a jacket reading “Fuck the Draft” in a corridor of the Los Angeles County Courthouse. The Supreme Court protected it, 5–4. Justice Harlan wrote that words are chosen as much for their emotive force as their cognitive force, and that the emotion is part of what speech carries. Also: “one man's vulgarity is another's lyric.”
  • 1972George Carlin records “Seven Words You Can Never Say on Television” and is arrested performing it at Milwaukee's Summerfest.
  • 1978FCC v. Pacifica. A New York radio station aired Carlin's follow-up, “Filthy Words,” at 2 p.m. The Court, 5–4, let the FCC regulate indecent broadcasting, mostly by time of day.
  • 2012FCC v. Fox. Unscripted one-off expletives on live TV. The Court ruled 8–0 for the broadcasters, but only because the rules were too vague to give them fair notice. It never reached free speech.
  • 2019Iancu v. Brunetti. A clothing brand called FUCT. The Court, 6–3, struck down the ban on registering “immoral or scandalous” trademarks as viewpoint discrimination.

The pattern: the law keeps trying to regulate the word, and keeps finding the offense isn't stored there. Cohen said the emotion is the message. Pacifica could only regulate by the clock. Fox couldn't define the rule. Brunetti couldn't ban the word without banning a point of view. Every case tries to grab the thing and comes up holding air.

Thesis

A word that achieved nothing

Lay the specimens side by side and one structure shows through. Every experiment separates the word's meaning from its force, and every time the force turns out to live somewhere else.

delete the lettersMeaning stays. The sting drops. (VII)
delete the whole wordThe gap fills with worse. (VII)
translate itMeaning travels. The sting stays home. (VIII)
invent a stand-inFunny, arousing, useless against pain. (IX)
overuse itThe charge drains. (IX, XI)
destroy the languageMeaning goes. The swear stays. (X)
outlaw itThe court grabs air. (XIII)

Fuck began as an act, lost the act, lost most of the insult, and ended up as nearly pure force with almost no content. It's defined mostly by what's been taken out of it, and by the sounds it refuses to make.

And what's left still runs on law. It keeps rhythm by rules nobody learned. It fills grammatical slots that hold nothing. It survives the death of the language around it. It slips past every court because there's nothing there to grab. A hollow word with perfect structure.

That's the same lesson as The Mad Science of Nothing, aimed at a word instead of the universe: measure the absence and the structure shows up.

Fuck is the via negativa of English.
