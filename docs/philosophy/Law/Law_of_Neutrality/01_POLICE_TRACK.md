## I. Ranadriel's Positions

From his X thread with Grok (Oct 2026) and the sessions that followed.

`[Ranadriel]` **Officers are trained personnel held to a high standard.** The public trusts them with that standard. Fitness, legal awareness, and split-second judgment are the job, and they should be spent protecting citizens, not ending a family pet's life without concrete reason. No blank checks.

`[Ranadriel]` **Injury risk is part of the job.** Like soldiers and firefighters, officers accept it. That acceptance is given the moment an officer enters a home carrying a loaded weapon.

`[Ranadriel]` **The lethal-force line should be imminent death or obvious fatality, not mere injury.**

`[Ranadriel]` **The home is different.** Outside a dwelling, normal rules. Inside a home: no fatal force against one or two dogs. Only at three or more does fatal force become potentially acceptable. Confined space changes the calculus, an officer's life is not realistically in danger from one or two dogs, and the cost of killing a family's animals can outweigh the cost of an officer taking a bite.

`[Ranadriel]` **The Smallwood shooting was unjustified.** He watched the bodycam: Max was trotting and curious, not charging.

## II. What the Law Is Now

- **The standard.** Force is a Fourth Amendment seizure judged by objective reasonableness from the officer's perspective in the moment — *Graham v. Connor*, 490 U.S. 386 (1989).
- **Dogs.** Multiple circuits treat killing a dog as a seizure of the owner's property ("effects"), reasonable only if the animal poses an imminent threat. Per the circuit consensus, a threat of *injury* is enough; officers have no duty to absorb a bite first. No circuit uses a bright line by number of dogs or by indoor/outdoor.
- **Qualified immunity.** An official is shielded from damages unless the right was "clearly established" when they acted — *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800 (1982). Courts may decide that prong without ever ruling on whether the Constitution was violated — *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S. 223 (2009).
- **The narrow exception.** An obvious enough violation can defeat immunity without a factually matching prior case — *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730 (2002); *Taylor v. Riojas*, 592 U.S. 7 (2020). It is rarely won.

## III. The Two Barriers

A family whose dog is shot has to clear two walls, and removing one does not remove the other.

**Barrier one — the reasonableness finding.** In *Brown v. Battle Creek Police Dep't*, 844 F.3d 556 (6th Cir. 2016), the Sixth Circuit held the right against unreasonable dog shootings clearly established, and still upheld the shootings as reasonable. Abolishing qualified immunity alone would not have changed that result. **The standard governing the conduct has to change too.**

**Barrier two — "clearly established."** Even a real violation pays nothing if no prior case was close enough. Because courts may skip the merits, the law can stay unsettled indefinitely and the next family meets the same wall.

## IV. Adopted Arguments

### Tombstone mentality — `[Adopted, 2026-10-22]`
Aviation has a name for the core flaw in qualified immunity: *tombstone mentality*, regulators who refuse to act on a known hazard until a crash and a body count force them to. The "clearly established law" test is built the same way. Liability cannot exist until a court has already ruled on a near-identical violation, so **the first victim of every new abuse pays the full price of setting the precedent, with no remedy.**

Aviation abandoned that design. It certifies against *foreseeable* failure and does not wait for the wreck. The amendment's argument is therefore not that the standard is too strict or too lenient. It is that the standard is **structurally reactive**, and every high-stakes field has already discarded reactive design as a known failure mode.

### Immunity as a trade, not a gift — `[Adopted, 2026-10-22, in Ranadriel's form]`
Aviation does grant immunity, but only in exchange for disclosure (the NASA Aviation Safety Reporting System; see [04_REMEDY_ARCHITECTURE.md](./04_REMEDY_ARCHITECTURE.md)). Qualified immunity gives the shield away for nothing: no disclosure, no report, no contribution to fixing the system. One can grant the premise that officers need protection and still show the current design buys nothing with it.

## V. Cautions on the Record

`[Proposed — Seed, 2026-10-13]` **The death-only threshold is the most attackable plank.** For human beings, deadly force is permitted on probable cause of a threat of *serious physical harm*, not only death — *Tennessee v. Garner*, 471 U.S. 1 (1985). A dog standard stricter than the human standard hands the defense an easy reply. Stronger ground is available:
- the indoor/outdoor distinction as a factual difference courts must weigh;
- state legislation, which can set use-of-force rules above the Fourth Amendment floor;
- a duty of restraint over the conduct *before* the shot (Section VI).

This is a caution, not a ruling. The threshold remains `[Open]`.

## VI. Next to Draft — The Restraint Clause `[Proposed — Seed, 2026-10-13]`

A reasonableness test that starts at the moment of the shot misses most of what went wrong. The restraint clause covers the period before it: what the officer knew, what danger was avoidable, what alternatives were available, what planning the situation called for.

The Palestine facts give it a concrete test (verified facts only):
- repeated warnings that dogs were inside;
- a door left open, through which the dogs came out;
- six children in the home;
- about eight shots fired inside a family home;
- the owner standing next to the dog, in the line of fire.

Settling this clause also narrows the other open decisions: the mental-state object has to attach to these same facts, and the death-only threshold is decided by whether the clause reaches Max or only a human life.
