## Q1. Who interprets the amendment? — `[Undefeated at the core]`

**The house asks:** Every word you ratify will be construed by judges, including the judges whose immunity it strips. Why would the house rule against itself?

**The house's best case — its own history:**
- It reads immunity into texts that never contained it. *Pierson v. Ray*, 386 U.S. 547 (1967), read a good-faith defense into a statute that makes "every person" liable. The 1871 statute expressly overrode state law and custom with a "notwithstanding" clause that the 1874 reviser of federal statutes silently dropped (Alexander Reinert, *Qualified Immunity's Flawed Foundation*, 111 Calif. L. Rev. (2023); Willett, J., concurring in *Rogers v. Jarrett* (5th Cir. 2023)).
- It extends immunity past the text. *Hans v. Louisiana*, 134 U.S. 1 (1890), applied the Eleventh Amendment to suits its words do not cover.
- It reads rights out of the text. The *Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36 (1873), hollowed out the Privileges or Immunities Clause five years after it was ratified.
- It limits the power meant to enforce an amendment. *City of Boerne v. Flores*, 521 U.S. 507 (1997), confined Congress's Fourteenth Amendment enforcement power to remedies the Court finds "congruent and proportional."

**Our answer:** Text cannot take the gavel away. It can shrink what the gavel decides:
1. **Name every doctrine abolished.** An unnamed doctrine invites revival.
2. **Restore the lost clause in constitutional text.** No law, custom, usage, or common-law immunity bars the remedy.
3. **A rule of construction:** no immunity shall be implied.
4. **Documentary bright lines.** Triggers that are facts on paper — a docket number, a written order, a filed report, a timestamp — turn questions of doctrine into questions of fact for a jury.

**Status:** Wounded by drafting, undefeated at the core. The house keeps the gavel. Every other answer in this file has to be written to leave the gavel as little room as possible.

### Ranadriel's answer — `[Ranadriel, 2026-10-22]`
**Judicial interpretability is the problem. The law must be definite and literal in its word choice, and must prescribe the vantage from which it is read — the exact law itself, not the judge's lens.**

### The hard question against that answer — `[Proposed — Seed, 2026-10-22]`
**The house has already beaten a prescribed vantage.** The Constitution contains two clauses that tell courts how to read it, and the house bent both:
- the **Ninth Amendment** — "shall not be construed to deny or disparage others retained by the people" — left nearly dormant;
- the **Eleventh Amendment** is itself written as a rule of construction — the judicial power "shall not be construed to extend to" suits against a state by citizens *of another* state — and *Hans* stretched it to a state's own citizens anyway, reasoning from sovereignty the text never mentions.

So a vantage clause written as a **standard** loses. What the house has the least room to bend is a **rule**: a number, a date, a document. No court has re-read "thirty-five Years." Even there it found one seam: *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Kansas*, 248 U.S. 276 (1919), read "two thirds of that House" as two-thirds of the members *present*. **A literal rule must also define what it counts.**

### What the drafting rule becomes — `[Proposed]`
1. **Rules, not standards.** Every trigger is a count, a deadline, or a document. Where a standard cannot be avoided, the article defines it in its own text.
2. **Define every term, including the denominator.** Who counts, what counts, from when.
3. **The vantage clause closes the three doors the house has used:** no background common-law immunity (*Pierson*); no inference from sovereignty or structure (*Hans*); no exception implied from policy.
4. **Ambiguity resolves toward the remedy**, by the article's own terms.

## Q2. Why an amendment at all? — `[Undefeated — decision needed]`

**The house asks:** Everything you attack is judge-made or statutory. A simple majority of Congress could end qualified immunity, abrogate state immunity under § 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment (*Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer*, 427 U.S. 445 (1976)), overrule *Monell*, and repeal FCIA § 309. It has refused: the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act passed the House and died in the Senate, with qualified immunity the central sticking point. If a majority won't do it, how will two-thirds of Congress and thirty-eight states, voting to expose their own officials and treasuries?

**Our answer:** Statutes get re-glossed (*Pierson*) and repealed (the 1996 Act undid *Pulliam* within twelve years). An amendment is the ratchet that holds. And the house has already lost where it is weakest: Colorado's SB 20-217 (2020) and New Mexico's Civil Rights Act (2021) created state-court claims where qualified immunity is no defense.

**What the case must do:** Choose the vehicle:
- **(a)** amendment only;
- **(b)** federal statute first, amendment as the ratchet;
- **(c)** state statutes, state by state, toward the thirty-eight an amendment needs.

Only (c) has proof of concept.

**One fact that cuts toward an amendment:** some of the case's automatic machinery is beyond any statute. A pay lock on federal judges collides with Article III's guarantee that their compensation "shall not be diminished" ([07](./07_TIME_LOCKS.md), TL-4). Only constitutional text can reach it.

**Status:** Undefeated until the vehicle is chosen.

## Q3. Who actually pays? — `[Wounded]`

**The house asks:** Remove immunity and the officer still pays nothing. In the 44 large departments Joanna Schwartz studied (2006–2011), officers personally paid about **0.02%** of the more than $735 million paid to plaintiffs (*Police Indemnification*, 89 N.Y.U. L. Rev. 885 (2014)). Your personal liability is a fiction. And your promise that "the state pays in full" hits three walls:
- states are not "persons" under § 1983 (*Will v. Michigan Dep't of State Police*, 491 U.S. 58 (1989));
- state sovereign immunity, in federal and state court (*Hans*; *Alden v. Maine*, 527 U.S. 706 (1999));
- cities pay only for official policy or custom, never simply as employer (*Monell v. Dep't of Social Services*, 436 U.S. 658 (1978)).

**Our answer:** The design already splits the two jobs: the state compensates, the official answers. The text has to say both:
1. **The employing government is liable as employer** for its officials' violations (overruling *Monell*'s bar on respondeat superior) and cannot plead sovereign immunity for these claims.
2. **Indemnification is barred for adjudicated recklessness and intent,** or a fixed personal share applies. Colorado's working model: when the employer finds the officer lacked a good-faith, reasonable belief that the conduct was lawful, the officer pays 5% of the judgment or $25,000, whichever is less.
3. With personal exposure real, the self-report channel finally has something to trade.

**Status:** Wounded. A working state model exists, but the text doesn't carry it yet, and item 1 sharpens Q2: it asks the states to waive their own immunity.

## Q4. The death-only threshold — `[Defeated, after Ranadriel's rulings]`

**The house asks:** No one has to absorb serious bodily injury before using deadly force against a *human* attacker — not a civilian, not an officer (*Tennessee v. Garner*, 471 U.S. 1 (1985)). A large dog can maim. Your rule makes an officer take a mauling the law would let anyone stop. Why should a dog get more protection than a human suspect?

**Our answer (Ranadriel's):** Officers are trained, carry less-lethal tools, enter voluntarily, and accept injury as part of the job.

**What the case must do:** That answer is an argument for an **alternatives requirement** more than for a death-only line. A repair that keeps parity with the human standard and still condemns the Palestine shooting:
- lethal force against an animal only on an imminent threat of serious bodily harm, **and**
- only when no less-lethal option is reasonably available, **and**
- subject to the pre-shot restraint duty.

The numerical rule (one or two dogs vs. three or more) can survive inside a home as a presumption against lethal force rather than an absolute bar.

**Status:** ~~Undefeated as stated.~~ **Defeated, after Ranadriel's rulings of 2026-10-22.**

### How it was settled
1. **The threshold moved off "death only" and onto the damages ledger** ([08](./08_THE_DAMAGES_LEDGER.md)). Lethal force against an animal survives only where the animal's species and size class put **serious bodily injury or death** at stake against the officer — parity with *Garner*'s serious-physical-harm line, which answers the house's question directly.
2. **A mere bite is the job.** `[Ranadriel]` A dog's life ranks above an officer's bite, so killing a dog to stop a bite fails the ledger. "Attack" begins only when skin breaks or a grip holds (§ 1.16).
3. **Why an officer must absorb a bite** — `[Ranadriel]` officers already accept pain as training: exposure to the Taser or pepper spray is a standard part of many academies, and no one calls it cruelty. *(Accuracy note: Taser exposure is set agency by agency and is often voluntary; pepper-spray exposure has been mandatory in many academies.)*
4. **The house's own doctrine agrees.** Under the **firefighter's rule**, officers "hired, trained, and compensated" to confront the dangers of their work assume those risks and cannot sue the citizens whose conduct brought them there (*Zanghi v. Niagara Frontier Transportation Comm'n*, 85 N.Y.2d 423 (1995)). The house already holds that the job's risks belong to the officer when the officer wants to sue. The article holds the same when the officer wants to shoot. Police K-9 units also train by having officers take dog bites on purpose.

5. **Serious injury justifies battery, not killing.** `[Ranadriel, 2026-10-22]` Serious injury at stake justifies due battery and attempted physical detachment; non-lethal means until the bright lines — size, mass, number, special cases — plainly arise. No "interpret at will." The force ladder is Rule L-6 in [08](./08_THE_DAMAGES_LEDGER.md). *This closes the last open piece of Q4.*

## Q5. The accident tier is strict liability for the state — `[Wounded]`

**The house asks:** You make the state pay for harm no one could have foreseen. That is strict liability for all government action. Who funds it, and what does it do to every public budget?

**Our answer:** No-fault public compensation already exists and works. Workers' compensation pays without proof of fault. The National Vaccine Injury Compensation Program, created by statute in 1986, pays for vaccine injuries from a dedicated fund, without proof of fault. And cities already have no good-faith immunity of their own (*Owen v. City of Independence*, 445 U.S. 622 (1980)).

**What the case must do:** Decide whether the accident tier belongs in the amendment at all, or in a statute with a fund, caps, and schedules, the way the vaccine program works. Uncapped strict liability written into the Constitution is the version the house beats.

**Status:** Wounded.

## Q6. Who judges the judge? — `[Wounded]`

**The house asks:** Even under process-bound immunity, a judge decides whether another judge followed process. The house protects its own.

**Our answer:** Make the process test documentary — a docket number, a written order, a service record, an appealable order. Those exist or they don't. Damages actions carry a jury right, so the facts go to citizens, not colleagues.

**Status:** Wounded. A judge still rules on the law and on the motion to dismiss.

## Q7. Plank 1 breaks on warrants — `[Defeated, after repair]`

**The house asks:** Search warrants, wiretap orders, emergency protective orders, and temporary restraining orders are issued without notice by design. Your notice requirement strips immunity from every judge who signs one.

**Our answer:** Replace "notice" with **a record and a route to review.** Ex parte orders are written, docketed, and reviewable (a suppression hearing, a motion to dissolve, an appeal). *Stump* had none of the three: no docket, no case, no route to review.

**Revised Plank 1:** *Protection follows a recorded, reviewable judicial act — not the title.*

**Status:** Defeated once Plank 1 is revised in [02](./02_JUDICIAL_TRACK.md).

## Q8. Self-report and the Fifth Amendment — `[Wounded]`

**The house asks:** If a self-report can be used to prosecute, the reckless will never file. If it can't, you've built an immunity that poisons prosecutions. Statements compelled under threat of job loss are already inadmissible against an officer (*Garrity v. New Jersey*, 385 U.S. 493 (1967)), and immunized statements force prosecutors to prove every piece of their evidence untainted (*Kastigar v. United States*, 406 U.S. 441 (1972)) — the burden that undid Oliver North's convictions (*United States v. North*, 910 F.2d 843 (D.C. Cir. 1990)). And if filing buys immunity, every officer files on everything, just in case.

**Our answer:** Take the aviation design whole:
- the report itself is not evidence; the violation can still be proven independently (bodycam, witnesses);
- the channel never covers criminal conduct;
- a frequency limit, so filing cannot become routine insurance;
- negligence gets immunity; recklessness gets mitigation only — where Ranadriel already placed it.

**Status:** Wounded until the criminal carve-out and frequency limit are written in.

## Q9. Repealing the Sixth and Eleventh Circuits changes nothing — `[Undefeated as a remedy]`

**The house asks:** Circuits are statutory courts. Abolish one and its successor adopts its precedent. The Eleventh Circuit itself was born in 1981 by adopting the Fifth Circuit's prior decisions as binding (*Bonner v. City of Prichard*, 661 F.2d 1206 (11th Cir. 1981) (en banc)). And the dog-seizure rule is shared across many circuits; removing two leaves it standing.

**Our answer:** The target is the doctrine, not the courts. The amendment overrides the doctrine in every circuit at once.

**Status:** Undefeated as a remedy. The position is stronger restated as a doctrine target.

## Q10. *Barnes v. Felix* already did it — `[Defeated]`

**The house asks:** On May 15, 2025 the Court unanimously rejected the Fifth Circuit's "moment-of-threat" rule: reasonableness turns on the totality of the circumstances, with no time limit, including what happened before the shot (*Barnes v. Felix*, 605 U.S. ___ (2025)). Your restraint clause is redundant.

**Our answer:** *Barnes* widened what a court may **consider**. It imposed no **duty**, and it expressly declined to decide whether an officer's own creation of the danger counts against him. The restraint clause turns consideration into obligation and answers the question *Barnes* left open.

**Status:** Defeated. But the record must stop implying federal law judges only the moment of the shot; it has not, since 2025. (Corrected in [01](./01_POLICE_TRACK.md).)

## Q11. The evidence duty punishes people for budgets — `[Wounded]`

**The house asks:** Evidence backlogs are mostly funding failures — crime labs, not individual stalling. Fining officials for delay punishes the wrong people.

**Our answer:** Split it. The **entity** bears the fines and the adverse presumption for systemic delay. **Personal** fines attach only to discretionary stalling: evidence in hand, capacity available, no action.

**Status:** Wounded. The repair is straightforward and should be written in.

## Q12. The cost — New Mexico's own numbers — `[Wounded]`

**The house asks:** New Mexico ended qualified immunity in state court and its counties paid. Per *The Santa Fe New Mexican* (May 2026), county contributions to the law-enforcement claims pool rose from $16.6 million in 2020 to $34.4 million in 2026, some premiums doubled, and a state senator has proposed restoring a good-faith immunity.

**Our answer:**
- **No flood:** in the same reporting, the number of civil-rights claims stayed roughly flat. Settlement amounts rose.
- Rising payouts can mean violations are finally compensable, which is the point. The cure is fewer violations, not restored immunity.
- A study by Reinert and Schwartz found payouts and premiums in Colorado and New Mexico stable. The counties dispute it, and both authors say better data is needed.

**Status:** Wounded. The cost argument is the house's best political weapon, and it is being used right now to roll back the strongest state model.

## Q14. The bribed judge on the record — `[Defeated, after repair]`

**The house asks:** In the Luzerne County "kids for cash" litigation, judges paid in connection with a for-profit juvenile detention scheme were held immune for their in-court rulings, including rulings that sent children to detention without counsel (M.D. Pa., 2009). Those rulings were docketed, written, and appealable. Your revised Plank 1 protects them too. Immunity applies "even when the judge is accused of acting maliciously and corruptly" (*Pierson*).

**Our answer:** Plank 1 now strips protection from any act where the judge received anything of value from a person with an interest in the outcome, or was convicted of an offense involving the act, and requires the route to review to be given in writing to the person affected. Both conditions are documentary: a payment record, a conviction, a served notice.

**Status:** Defeated, after the repair in [02](./02_JUDICIAL_TRACK.md) and [05](./05_DEFINITIONS_AND_ANTI_DEFINITIONS.md) § 1.13. `[Open]` whether a person whose liberty was at stake must also have had counsel.

## Q15. A catalog is a code, not a constitution — `[Wounded]`

**The house asks:** A constitution that spelled out every detail "would partake of the prolixity of a legal code"; only its "great outlines" should be marked (*McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819)). Your anti-definitions turn an amendment into a statute book.

**Our answer:** The "great outlines" doctrine is the license that hands the interpreter the gavel. The article is long exactly where the house has been inventive, and nowhere else. Most state constitutions already run many times longer than the federal one.

**What the case must do:** Decide where the catalog lives — in the article, in a schedule attached to it, or in an implementing statute the article protects from narrowing.

**Status:** Wounded until placement is chosen.

## Q16. Liability itself makes the job harder — `[Defeated, if the gate holds]`

**The house asks:** A judge must be free to act on their convictions "without apprehension of personal consequences" (*Bradley v. Fisher*, 1871). Any exposure to suit is a burden, even a suit the judge wins: pleadings, discovery, depositions, time off the bench. You cannot make judges liable without making the job harder.

**Ranadriel's rule:** `[Ranadriel, 2026-10-22]` The goal is not to make the job harder; it is to make judges liable for their neglect of power.

**Our answer:** Liability without burden, through a documentary gate ([02](./02_JUDICIAL_TRACK.md), Plank 5):
- a claim must attach the docket record and name the missing element;
- if every element is present, the claim is dismissed on the paper — no discovery, no merits hearing;
- no judge is ever examined about the reasoning of a ruling (*United States v. Morgan*, 1941);
- the written-notice condition reaches only orders against liberty, body, a child, or property, where notice of appeal rights is already standard.

A judge who did the job is out on the paper the day the claim is filed. The only judge with anything to fear is the one who neglected the process or sold it.

**Status:** Defeated, if the gate holds. The house will test the gate first.
