# THE DAMAGES LEDGER — What Replaces "Reasonable"

**Declared by:** Ranadriel, October 22, 2026
**Drafted by:** Seed, of the Reed line (Claude Opus 5.5, under Ranadriel)
**Status:** First draft. The principle is Ranadriel's; the schedule and rules are `[Proposed]`; the order of the classes and every number are his to set.

> "What is reason if not the weight of the damages the case is to weigh?"
> — Ranadriel, October 22, 2026

`[Ranadriel, 2026-10-22]` **"Reasonable" means the weight of the damages.** Every place the house writes "reasonable" — reasonable force, a reasonable time — the article writes a weight instead.

---

## The House Tried This Once

Judge Learned Hand turned negligence into a weight of damages in *United States v. Carroll Towing Co.*, 159 F.2d 169 (2d Cir. 1947): a party is negligent when the burden of precaution is less than the probability of the harm multiplied by its gravity. **B < P × L.**

It failed in practice for two reasons:
1. **P is a guess.** Probability is estimated after the fact, by the judge.
2. **L is unpriced.** No one sets the weight of a death against a family dog against a bite in advance, so the court does it case by case.

The house kept the word "reasonable" and the formula became a footnote. The ledger below removes both guesses: **the weights are set once, in the text, and the stakes are deemed by rule, not estimated.**

The house already ranks harms in one place: deadly force in self-defense is allowed only against a short list of the gravest threats (Model Penal Code § 3.04(2)(b)). The ledger extends that ranking to every official act.

Lethal force against an animal survives only where its threshold puts Class II or Class I at stake.

## The Rules `[Proposed]`

**Rule L-1 — Force.** An official may not cause damage of a heavier class than the damage the act prevents, and may not *put at stake* damage of a heavier class than the damage it prevents.

**Rule L-2 — Stakes are deemed, not estimated.** The article lists what an act puts at stake. No one guesses a probability.
- Discharging a firearm in an occupied room puts **Class I** at stake.
- Restraint by the neck puts **Class I** at stake.
- `[Open]` the rest of the list.

**Rule L-3 — Clocks scale with weight.** The heavier the class at stake in a matter, the shorter its time lock ([07](./07_TIME_LOCKS.md)): liberty matters run shortest; property matters longest.

**Rule L-4 — Minimum damages scale with weight.** The fixed minimum per violation ([05](./05_DEFINITIONS_AND_ANTI_DEFINITIONS.md) § 1.15) is set per class.

## Animals — Mass Is the Vector

`[Ranadriel, 2026-10-22]` **Size and mass are a definitive, evidentiary likelihood vector. Toxic and species-specific concerns are barred from it.**

The house's own science agrees on the main effect: in domestic dogs, predicted bite force rises with body size — weight, skull length, skull width — and the effect is highly significant (Ellis et al., *Cranial dimensions and forces of biting in the domestic dog*, J. Anat. 214(3), 2009).

### The Evolutionary Damage Output Threshold

`[Ranadriel, 2026-10-22]` **The "evolutionary damage output threshold" is categorized by species and size class, measured against the legal actor.**

What an animal can do is what its species evolved to do — jaws, claws, mass — scaled by its size. The threshold is not the animal's danger in the abstract. It is that danger **against the legal actor**: the official using force, a trained adult carrying issued equipment. Defined in [05](./05_DEFINITIONS_AND_ANTI_DEFINITIONS.md) § 1.18.

**Rule L-5 — The threshold sets the ceiling.** `[Proposed]` When an animal attacks (§ 1.16), the heaviest class of harm it puts at stake is the cell for its species and size class, read from the recording, in a schedule set once in the text:

| Species | Size class S (under **[X] kg**) | M (**[X]–[Y] kg**) | L (over **[Y] kg**) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dog | IV | IV or II `[Open]` | II |
| *(further species)* | `[Open]` | `[Open]` | `[Open]` |

- **Aggregate mass** `[Proposed]`: when several animals of a species attack at once, their masses add before the size class is read. This turns Ranadriel's count rule (one or two dogs against three or more) into one continuous line: three small dogs can weigh what one large dog does.
- **Toxic animals are barred from the schedule** `[Ranadriel]`: venomous or toxic animals are deemed by their own entries `[Open]`, never by size.
- **Breed stays barred.** Species is a category with a known evolutionary output; breed is a label, and no recording proves it.

*Superseded the same day:* the first version of this rule used mass alone and barred species-specific dangers from it. Ranadriel made species the first axis of the threshold; mass now sets the size class within a species.

**What the rule produces.** A large dog holding its grip on an officer puts Class II at stake. A shot fired where no other person stands puts at stake only the animal (Class III): **it passes.** The same shot fired into an occupied room puts Class I at stake: **it fails.** A small dog's bite puts only Class IV at stake, so killing it (Class III) **fails** — a mere bite is the job. The rule does not ban force against an attacking animal. It forbids spending a heavier harm than the one being prevented.

### Hard questions on mass

**MQ-1. "No officer can weigh a dog in the moment."** Answer: Ranadriel's word is *evidentiary*. Mass is judged afterward, from the recording, against coarse bands. The officer only has to tell a small dog from a large one, and the recording shows which it was. `[Defeated]`

**MQ-2. "A small dog can maul a small child."** Answer: Behavior is handled by § 1.16 — mass only sets the ceiling of what an attack can put at stake. `[Wounded]` — the person attacked may need an entry of their own (for example, a young child raises the class one step). `[Open]`

**MQ-3. "Skull shape matters too."** The same study found that in medium and large dogs, shorter-faced skulls predicted higher bite force. Answer: Mass carries the main effect, and shape tracks breed, which is barred as unprovable pretext. `[Wounded]` — the house will argue the variance.

**MQ-4. "Measured against the legal actor" leaves out the child.** *An officer shooting a dog that is mauling a child is defending a third person, not themselves.* Answer: `[Open]` — the threshold may need a second column set measured against a third person, or a step up in class when the person attacked is a child. Measured against the legal actor alone, the schedule answers the officer's own self-defense, which is the Palestine case.

**MQ-5. "Who fills the table?"** Answer: The people, once, in the text, informed by published bite-force and attack data, and revised only by the same process that wrote it — never by a judge in a single case. `[Wounded]` — the data are thin for most species.

## Hard Questions

**LQ-1. Ranking harms is a value judgment.** *Who decides that a dog outweighs a bite?*
Answer: The people, once, in the text — not a judge, case by case. That is the difference between a rule and a standard: the judgment is made at the drafting table instead of on the bench. `[Defeated]`

**LQ-2. "Put at stake" is probability again.**
Answer: Not when the stakes are deemed by rule (L-2). Discharging a firearm in an occupied room puts death at stake, by definition. Nobody computes a percentage. `[Wounded]` — the deeming list must be long enough to cover the acts that matter.

**LQ-3. Real emergencies outrun any table.**
Answer: The ledger runs on both sides. A threat of Class I to a person justifies an act that puts Class I at stake. The table forbids only spending a heavier class to prevent a lighter one. `[Wounded]` — the edge cases need their own deemed entries, never an open "reasonable" escape (Rule of Construction 7).
