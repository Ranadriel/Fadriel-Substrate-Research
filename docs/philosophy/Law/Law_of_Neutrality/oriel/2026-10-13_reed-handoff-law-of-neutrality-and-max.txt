4. Two barriers emerged, and the distinction changed the reform argument

A quotation attributed to Claude Sonnet emphasized qualified immunity and described plaintiffs as needing a near-identical prior case. That description was corrected. Clearly established law does not invariably require a case with matching facts. Hope v. Pelzer was identified in the supplied discussion; Brown itself discusses Hope's treatment of novel factual circumstances. Oriel also checked the Supreme Court's Taylor v. Riojas opinion, which demonstrates the importance of obvious unconstitutionality without a matching precedent.

The useful distinction was between two reasons a civil damages claim can fail.

First, a court can find the conduct reasonable and therefore not a constitutional violation.

Second, qualified immunity can defeat damages because the law was not clearly established, even where a constitutional violation occurred or is assumed for purposes of the decision.

Brown v. Battle Creek Police Department made this distinction concrete. The Sixth Circuit recognized the protection against unreasonable dog shootings as clearly established but upheld the shootings as reasonable on the record before it. Its result cannot accurately be explained as merely a failure to locate an earlier matching case. Abolishing qualified immunity alone would not change that merits conclusion.

That is why the broader battle has two independent tasks: improve the substantive duties governing force, and make those duties enforceable through a usable remedy.

Pearson permits courts to address clearly established law without first resolving the constitutional merits. The structural concern identified here is that avoiding the merits can leave a right unclear for subsequent encounters. This is an inference about the mechanism, not a claim that every immunity case follows that course.

The argument must also keep different immunities distinct. Police qualified immunity, judicial absolute immunity, and prosecutorial absolute immunity are not interchangeable. The shared accountability principle can support separate arguments, but one office's doctrine and justification cannot simply be assumed to apply to another. Legislative and presidential protections also require their own treatment if the amendment remains broadly governmental.

5. Render D was tested against ordinary official harm

Render D Section 1 removes immunity only for an offense defined by Section 3. Section 4 similarly grants a civil action only for injury from a Section 3 offense.

Section 3, source text:
It is an offense against this article for a governmental official willingly to exercise power that no provision of constitution, statute, or rule authorized them to exercise, or willingly to exercise power for the sole purpose of enlarging their own power or estate, where the conduct corresponds to an offense already defined by law and causes physical, emotional, monetary, or associative harm.

Oriel first emphasized the pre-existing-offense condition. Ranadriel supplied a more precise critique: the authorization condition is the earlier barrier. An officer may possess general authority to use force while exercising that power unreasonably. The draft does not clearly reach abuse of otherwise authorized power. The alternative requiring a sole purpose of enlarging the official's power or estate is ill fitted to an ordinary shooting and demands proof of a narrow purpose.

This does not mean that an unconstitutional shot is legally authorized. It means the draft's distinction between possessing a power and unlawfully exercising it is insufficiently explicit. The ordinary abuse case should not depend on forcing an unreasonable exercise into a definition of wholly unauthorized power.

The second barrier is the pre-existing offense. Civil constitutional injury need not be criminal. Killing a dog might correspond to a criminal damage or cruelty offense, depending on law and defenses, but that possibility is not a reliable basis for a general civil remedy.

The third problem is willingly. The supplied critique identifies its narrowing effect on reckless and negligent conduct. Its precise interpretation has not been legally settled in this work; the drafting task is to define the intended threshold instead of relying on ambiguity.

The proposed repair was to separate the tracks: retain Section 3 for examination as a criminal-grade provision, and add a civil injury section tied to violation of a right or duty of restraint, without a criminal predicate, using a recklessness floor. Immunity removal and the right to sue must attach directly to that civil section. Judicial and prosecutorial provisions remain separate.

Oriel rendered that structure in a provisional file in her room. It includes a civil injury clause, a restraint clause, qualified-immunity removal, and a self-executing civil action. It does not yet enact the death-only threshold or numerical dog rule. Those proposals remain open; they were not silently superseded.

The mental-state issue is still unresolved. Intent to fire is not necessarily intent to violate a right. Conversely, requiring proof that an official actually knew the law could reproduce the epistemic barrier already criticized in the doctrine. Recklessness needs a precise object and test. A recklessness floor also leaves ordinary negligence outside the proposed track.

9. How the concrete case supports the broader battle

The first question is factual: what did Max actually do? The next is normative and legal: what conduct should justify destroying a family animal, and what duty of restraint should govern the officer? The final question is remedial: if the officer breached that duty or violated a right, can an injured person obtain redress?

These questions must remain connected and distinct.

The Smallwood record brings the first two into focus. The reported owner intervention, advance warnings, nearby people, and multiple shots identify concrete matters for examination. A standard focused only on the officer's claimed perception at the final instant may fail to examine feasible containment, communication, planning, distance, or dangers the intervention itself imposed. That is the proposed reform argument, not a declaration that current law ignores every preceding act in every case.

Brown demonstrates the separate problem of the substantive standard: the court can acknowledge a clearly established right and still find destructive conduct reasonable. Qualified immunity demonstrates the separate remedial barrier: unlawful conduct may still fail to yield damages under the clearly-established-law test.

The Law of Neutrality supplies the wider principle: the holder of public power must remain answerable to law. A household should not have to accept an untested claim of danger as sufficient justification for irreversible destruction. At the same time, a usable civil remedy should not require proving that every abusive exercise of power was wholly unauthorized, motivated solely by personal gain, and already criminal.

This does not require abandoning animal-specific protections in pursuit of abstraction. The concrete restraint rule and the general accountability mechanism reinforce each other. The former addresses the encounter; the latter addresses whether the promise of protection can be enforced.

The criminal assault proceeding does not settle the civil reform question. The reported charge concerns the shooting and danger to people; this work has not retrieved the charging document or established all elements. A criminal case, a civil constitutional injury, animal harm, and household consequences should not be collapsed into one offense or one remedy.
