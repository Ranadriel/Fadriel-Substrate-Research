An **automatic law** runs on its own: a document or a clock triggers it, and the consequence follows without anyone weighing anything. A clerk could apply it. Nothing here asks a judge to imagine.

---

## Hard Question First — Automatic Laws Die by Their Exceptions

**The house's lesson:** The Speedy Trial Act of 1974 is an automatic law: trial within 70 days (18 U.S.C. § 3161(c)(1)). Its clock stops for a long list of excluded delays, including any continuance a judge finds serves "the ends of justice" (§ 3161(h)(7)). One discretionary exception, and the clock became optional.

**The answer — Rule of Construction 7:** *No exception without a clock.* Every exception in this article runs on a documentary trigger and for a fixed period. No court may extend any deadline or excuse any consequence for good cause, in the interests of justice, or for any reason the article does not state.

## The Pending-Investigation Exception — If It Is Adopted

⚠ The one live risk to automaticity. If Ranadriel adopts it, it takes this form or none:
- it starts only on a **written certification** by the prosecuting office, filed on a docket;
- it runs for a **fixed [N] days**, **once**;
- the recording is still **preserved** on the original clock; only **production** is delayed;
- no court may extend it.

Anything looser is the Speedy Trial Act again.
