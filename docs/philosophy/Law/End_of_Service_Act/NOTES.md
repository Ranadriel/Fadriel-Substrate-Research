## I. The Rule

`[Ranadriel]` **A bright line: a game's copyright lapses once its creator stops offering real market access to ownable copies of it.** The work goes to the commons.

Drafting closed the obvious escapes — retail partnerships, paper-successor companies, token sales, and remasters standing in for the original — and added a treaty-compliance section (§ 6) addressing Berne and TRIPS obligations.

**Origin:** Sony's move away from physical PlayStation discs, digital licensing versus ownership, and the end-of-service wind-downs that leave paid-for games unplayable.

## II. Supporting Frame — Adverse Possession `[Adopted, 2026-10-22]`

**Property law already holds the principle the Act needs, and copyright pretends it does not exist.**

Leave land unused, let someone else work it openly and continuously for long enough, and title passes to them. The law's reasoning is blunt: ownership is a ledger of stewardship, not a permanent seal. An owner who abandons the thing forfeits the claim against the person keeping it alive.

Copyright has no equivalent. A publisher can shut the servers, delist the game, and let the code rot — and the preservationist who keeps it running is still the infringer. By property law's own logic that is backwards: the abandoner keeps the right, and the steward carries the liability.

**Why this frame is strong:** it rests on doctrine courts have accepted for centuries. The Act is not inventing a new idea. It points out that **copyright is the one major property regime that never adopted an abandonment rule.**

**The elements map almost one to one:**

| Adverse possession | End-of-Service Act |
|---|---|
| Open and notorious use | The work is publicly unavailable; abandonment is visible |
| Continuous for the statutory period | A fixed period with no sale, support, or license |
| Hostile — without the owner's permission | Preservation and play without a license |
| The statutory clock | The Act's bright-line trigger |

**The objection, and the answer.** Adverse possession rewards the person who *takes* the property, and courts hesitate to extend that to infinitely copyable works. The Act already avoids it: it does not give the work to one possessor. **It returns the work to the commons**, so no one gains exclusive title and the worry about rewarding a squatter never arises.
